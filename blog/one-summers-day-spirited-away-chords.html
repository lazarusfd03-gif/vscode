01 — THE SONG

A memory that starts in the middle of a thought

Before a single spirit appears in Spirited Away, this piece has already told you how the film will feel: a quiet piano, a melody that keeps climbing and sighing, and an ache for something that hasn't been lost yet.

"One Summer's Day" is the first track on the soundtrack and the film's opening theme. It plays as ten-year-old Chihiro slumps in the back seat of her family's car, moving to a new town and clutching a farewell bouquet from the friends she's leaving behind. The Japanese title, Ano Natsu e, literally means "to that summer". It's written as a look back, even though the story hasn't started yet. That double sense of time is the whole emotional trick of the piece.

Think of it as early-morning music: it lowers your shoulders and puts you somewhere warm and slightly sad. It's ideal for focused writing sessions, for winding down after a mix, or as the emotional reset before an orchestral project.

The story behind it

Joe Hisaishi had been Miyazaki's composer since Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind (1984), and by 2001 the two had a well-worn working method. Hisaishi first writes an image album, a set of sketches and songs based on Miyazaki's concept notes, before the animation is finished. Miyazaki listens and reacts, and the final score grows out of the themes that survive. The Spirited Away Image Album came out on 4 April 2001, three months before the soundtrack.

The finished score was recorded with the New Japan Philharmonic at Sumida Triphony Hall and at Wonder Station in Tokyo. Hisaishi conducted, played the piano and produced the album himself. The soundtrack was released by Studio Ghibli Records / Tokuma Japan Communications on 11 July 2001, nine days before the film opened in Japan.

The film went on to become the highest-grossing film in Japanese history for almost two decades and won the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature. The music did well too. The soundtrack won the Annie Award for Music in a Feature Production, Animation Album of the Year at the Japan Gold Disc Awards, and Best Music at the Tokyo International Anime Fair. "One Summer's Day" became Hisaishi's most famous composition.

The second life: "The Name of Life"

The melody was too singable to stay instrumental. With lyrics by poet-lyricist Wakako Kaku (who also wrote the words to the end-credits song "Always With Me"), it became "The Name of Life" (いのちの名前, Inochi no Namae). The vocal version was later taken up by Ayaka Hirahara, Hisaishi's daughter Mai Fujisawa in concert, and more recently YOASOBI's Lilas Ikuta.

02 — WHY IT WORKS

Three harmonic habits that make it sound like Hisaishi

It starts on IV, not I. The core harmony opens on Fmaj7, not C. Starting away from home makes the music feel as if it's already in motion, like a memory you've dropped into halfway through. The tonic only arrives in bar 3, and even then as a soft Cmaj7 rather than a plain triad.

Major 7ths everywhere. Fmaj7 and Cmaj7 each have a note a semitone below the octave, a gentle friction that sounds bittersweet instead of happy. That's the "nostalgia" colour.

The borrowed minor chord. At the end of the first phrase, F major turns into Fm7. That chord is borrowed from C minor, and its A♭ sighs down to G. In the chorus, Cm/E♭ → Dm7 → D♭7 → Dm7/G slides chromatically in the bass. This is where the melody's "ache" comes from.

Lazante reimagining: enhanced · cinematic orchestral · calm

The approach: keep Hisaishi's bass motion and melody intact, then deepen every chord with 9ths, 11ths and Lydian ♯11s, and add two borrowed chords for widescreen colour. Nothing pushes the tempo or the dynamics. The aim is a slow bloom, not a climax.

Notice the outer voices. The bass walks F → E → D → G by step, while Violins I move G → D → C → A. Every chord keeps at least one common tone with the next, so the bed shifts like light rather than changing like slides. The B♭maj9/D chord comes from a published analysis of a later Hisaishi concert arrangement, whose intro moves through Fmaj9, Cmaj9/E and B♭maj9/D.

Who they are

Joe Hisaishi

Composer · conductor · pianist

Born Mamoru Fujisawa in Nagano in 1950. His stage name is a nod to Quincy Jones. He started in minimalist and electronic music before scoring nearly every Miyazaki film from Nausicaä onward, and also Takeshi Kitano's films. His trademarks are piano-led themes, major-7th warmth and orchestration that leaves space.

Hayao Miyazaki

Director · writer

Co-founder of Studio Ghibli. He wrote and directed Spirited Away, and his concept notes are what Hisaishi's image albums start from.

Toshio Suzuki

Production producer

Studio Ghibli's long-time producer and Miyazaki's closest creative partner on the business side. He's credited as production producer on the soundtrack.

New Japan Philharmonic

Orchestra

A Tokyo orchestra founded in 1972. Its home base is Sumida Triphony Hall, where the Spirited Away score was recorded, and it has a long working relationship with Hisaishi.

Shigeki Fujino

Mastering engineer

A Universal Music Japan mastering engineer who has worked on Hisaishi's catalogue for over two decades, from the 2001 soundtrack to the 2023 Symphonic Celebration album.

Wakako Kaku

Lyricist ("The Name of Life")

A poet and lyricist who wrote the words for both of the film's songs, "Always With Me" and "The Name of Life", as well as lyrics for Hisaishi's "Merry-Go-Round of Life".
